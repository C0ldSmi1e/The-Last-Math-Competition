## Formal semantic obligations

1. **Surface and genus.** Give genuine mathematical definitions of closed surface and genus. The usual reading is a compact, connected, boundaryless orientable 2-manifold with genus g in the natural numbers. A counterexample may use an explicitly constructed genus-one torus, but its status as a closed surface of genus one must be established, not merely asserted by a label or a field chosen to be one.
2. **Convex polyhedron.** Use an ordinary geometric definition in Euclidean 3-space: a suitable finite polyhedron that is convex, compact, and three-dimensional, with an actual boundary surface. These properties may follow from the definition or be proved for a purported realization. Do not replace convexity by a numerical Euler relation or a bespoke predicate incorporating the desired conclusion.
3. **Realization.** State the relationship between the given surface and the polyhedron. The natural topological interpretation is a homeomorphism from the surface to the polyhedron's entire boundary. Establish the necessary geometric/topological bridge from the chosen definitions. Isometry is a stronger interpretation and needs a metric on the input surface, which the source does not provide.
4. **Edges and lengths.** Define actual edges/faces and vertices of that polyhedron, not an unrelated graph. Every edge's Euclidean length must be the real-number image of a natural prime; the length map must be injective on distinct edges. Arbitrary scaling does not automatically preserve this condition. These obligations can be discarded in a disproof only after showing that no underlying convex realization of a witness surface exists at all.
5. **Vertex count.** Interpret O(g) with an explicit uniform constant and eventual threshold (or state and justify another interpretation). A proof requires a bound independent of the particular surface and genus, over the quantified family. A disproof that excludes every convex realization of a fixed surface is independent of this bound.
6. **Logical coverage.** Formalize the universal assertion, or a demonstrably weaker necessary consequence whose failure implies its negation. If using a necessary consequence, prove the implication from the genuinely defined original objects. A contradictory pair of assumed integer identities is not by itself a disproof of this geometric assertion.

## Material ambiguities

- **Ambient space and boundary:** Ordinary usage suggests a convex polyhedron in R^3 and realization by its boundary. A filled three-dimensional solid cannot itself be a 2-manifold realization. Higher-dimensional embeddings or realization by an arbitrary subset of a polyhedron are different assertions and must not be silently substituted.
- **Polyhedron conventions:** “Polyhedron” can include unbounded or lower-dimensional objects in some formal libraries. A proof must either use the standard bounded solid convention or prove that its formal interpretation of a closed-surface realization removes the unwanted cases. An elementary fact about boundaries of compact full-dimensional convex bodies cannot be applied without those hypotheses.
- **Orientability and connectedness:** “Genus” ordinarily means orientable genus for connected closed surfaces, but nonorientable genus and disconnected surfaces are not expressly excluded. A valid genus-one orientable torus counterexample survives any reading that includes ordinary orientable closed surfaces.
- **Realization category:** No given metric is specified, making a topological/polyhedral realization the natural reading. A claimed proof cannot exploit an invented weak relation called “realizes.”
- **Big-O at zero:** Literal V <= Cg for every g, including zero, is stronger than standard asymptotic O(g) and is impossible at g = 0 for trivial counting reasons. That convention must not be used as an unannounced shortcut. A geometric obstruction for a positive-genus surface avoids this issue.
- **Units:** Numerical primality of physical lengths presumes a fixed Euclidean unit, or an explicitly existential choice of overall scale. This matters to a construction, but not to a disproof excluding convex realizations before imposing lengths.
